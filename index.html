Abstract

Fine-grained recognition of aquatic species is challenged by extreme long-tailed distributions, where rare species are severely underrepresented. We present FISHER, a gradient-decoupled hierarchical multi-task learning framework that structures species recognition around the natural biological hierarchy of aquatic organisms by decomposing the task into three interconnected components: anatomical part segmentation, morphological characteristic prediction, and species identification.

Unlike conventional multi-task approaches, FISHER maintains strictly unidirectional information flow through gradient detachment at task boundaries, preventing high-level classification objectives from corrupting lower-level morphological representations. Precise delineation of anatomical structures is achieved via learnable prototypes with orthogonality constraints, while homoscedastic uncertainty weighting dynamically equilibrates contributions from segmentation and classification tasks during training.

Evaluated on the Fish-Vista benchmark, FISHER achieves 97.7% mAP for unseen trait identification and delivers a 13.4% accuracy improvement for ultra-rare species over strong baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of hierarchical gradient decoupling for long-tailed fine-grained recognition.
